@sim c-domains-0
Describe the domain of $f(x,y)=\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2+y^2}$.
@s What could break?
It is a fraction, so the bottom cannot be zero:
$$(x-1)^2+y^2\neq 0$$
@s When is the bottom zero?
Two squares added together. Each is $\ge0$, so the sum is $0$ only if both are $0$:

$x-1=0$ and $y=0$, so $x=1$, $y=0$.
@s Describe it
Only the point $(1,0)$ is banned. Everything else is fine.
{{fig:punct10}}
@a $\text{dom}(f)=\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(1,0)\}$, the whole plane except the point $(1,0)$.

@sim c-domains-1
Describe the domain of $f(x,y)=\sqrt{x+y}$.
@s What could break?
A square root. The inside must not be negative:
$$x+y\ge 0$$
@s Rearrange
$$y\ge -x$$
@s Describe it
$y=-x$ is the diagonal line going down to the right. $y\ge-x$ means "on the line, or above it".
{{fig:halfplaneneg}}
@a $\text{dom}(f)=\{(x,y)\mid y\ge -x\}$, the half-plane on and above the line $y=-x$.

@sim c-domains-2
Describe the domain of $f(x,y)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{9-x^2-y^2}}$.
@s What could break?
Square root: inside $\ge 0$.

Fraction: bottom $\neq 0$.
@s Combine them
Together that means strictly positive:
$$9-x^2-y^2>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^2+y^2<9$$
@s Describe it
$x^2+y^2=9$ is a circle, centre $(0,0)$, radius $3$ (because $3^2=9$).

$<9$ means strictly inside. The circle itself is not included: an **open disk**.
{{fig:opendisk3}}
@a $\text{dom}(f)=\{(x,y)\mid x^2+y^2<9\}$, the inside of the circle of radius 3 (not the edge).

@sim c-limits-0
Find $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2+2y^2-6}{x^2+y^2+3}$.
@s Try plugging in
Check the bottom first: $0+0+3=3$. Not zero, so we can just plug in.
@s Plug in
$$\frac{0+0-6}{0+0+3}=-2$$
@a $-2$

@sim c-limits-1
Find $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(2,1)}\frac{xy-2y+3x-6}{x-2}$.
@s Try plugging in
Top: $2-2+6-6=0$. Bottom: $2-2=0$.

We get $\frac00$, so simplify first.
@s Factorise the top by grouping
$$xy-2y+3x-6=y(x-2)+3(x-2)=(x-2)(y+3)$$
@s Cancel the matching bracket
$$\frac{(x-2)(y+3)}{x-2}=y+3$$
@s Now plug in
$$1+3=4$$
@a $4$

@sim c-limits-2
Show that $f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}$ has no limit as $(x,y)\to(0,0)$.
@s Plan
Find two paths into $(0,0)$ that give different answers.
@s Path 1: along the x-axis
Put $y=0$:
$$f(x,0)=\frac{x^2}{x^2}=1$$
@s Path 2: along the y-axis
Put $x=0$:
$$f(0,y)=\frac{-y^2}{y^2}=-1$$
@s Compare
$1\neq-1$. Two paths, two different answers, so there is no limit.
@a The limit does not exist (it is $1$ along the $x$-axis and $-1$ along the $y$-axis).
