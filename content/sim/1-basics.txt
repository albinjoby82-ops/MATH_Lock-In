@sim c-domains-0
Describe the domain of $f(x,y)=\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2+y^2}$.
@s What could break?
It is a fraction, so the bottom cannot be zero:
$$(x-1)^2+y^2\neq 0$$
@s When is the bottom zero?
Two squares added together. Each is $\ge0$, so the sum is $0$ only if both are $0$:

$x-1=0$ and $y=0$, so $x=1$, $y=0$.
@s Describe it
Only the point $(1,0)$ is banned. Everything else is fine.
{{fig:punct10}}
@a $\text{dom}(f)=\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(1,0)\}$, the whole plane except the point $(1,0)$.

@sim c-domains-1
Describe the domain of $f(x,y)=\sqrt{x+y}$.
@s What could break?
A square root. The inside must not be negative:
$$x+y\ge 0$$
@s Rearrange
$$y\ge -x$$
@s Describe it
$y=-x$ is the diagonal line going down to the right. $y\ge-x$ means "on the line, or above it".
{{fig:halfplaneneg}}
@a $\text{dom}(f)=\{(x,y)\mid y\ge -x\}$, the half-plane on and above the line $y=-x$.

@sim c-domains-2
Describe the domain of $f(x,y)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{9-x^2-y^2}}$.
@s What could break?
Square root: inside $\ge 0$.

Fraction: bottom $\neq 0$.
@s Combine them
Together that means strictly positive:
$$9-x^2-y^2>0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^2+y^2<9$$
@s Describe it
$x^2+y^2=9$ is a circle, centre $(0,0)$, radius $3$ (because $3^2=9$).

$<9$ means strictly inside. The circle itself is not included: an **open disk**.
{{fig:opendisk3}}
@a $\text{dom}(f)=\{(x,y)\mid x^2+y^2<9\}$, the inside of the circle of radius 3 (not the edge).

@sim c-limits-0
Find $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{x^2+2y^2-6}{x^2+y^2+3}$.
@s Try plugging in
Check the bottom first: $0+0+3=3$. Not zero, so we can just plug in.
@s Plug in
$$\frac{0+0-6}{0+0+3}=-2$$
@a $-2$

@sim c-limits-1
Find $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(2,1)}\frac{xy-2y+3x-6}{x-2}$.
@s Try plugging in
Top: $2-2+6-6=0$. Bottom: $2-2=0$.

We get $\frac00$, so simplify first.
@s Factorise the top by grouping
$$xy-2y+3x-6=y(x-2)+3(x-2)=(x-2)(y+3)$$
@s Cancel the matching bracket
$$\frac{(x-2)(y+3)}{x-2}=y+3$$
@s Now plug in
$$1+3=4$$
@a $4$

@sim c-limits-2
Show that $f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2}$ has no limit as $(x,y)\to(0,0)$.
@s Plan
Find two paths into $(0,0)$ that give different answers.
@s Path 1: along the x-axis
Put $y=0$:
$$f(x,0)=\frac{x^2}{x^2}=1$$
@s Path 2: along the y-axis
Put $x=0$:
$$f(0,y)=\frac{-y^2}{y^2}=-1$$
@s Compare
$1\neq-1$. Two paths, two different answers, so there is no limit.
@a The limit does not exist (it is $1$ along the $x$-axis and $-1$ along the $y$-axis).

@sim c-continuity-0
At what points $(x,y)$ is $f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2-4)$ continuous?
@s What could break?
$\ln$ needs a positive input:
$$x^2+y^2-4>0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x^2+y^2>4$$
@s Describe it
$x^2+y^2=4$ is the circle of radius $2$ (because $2^2=4$).

$>4$ means strictly **outside** it. The circle itself is not included.
{{fig:outdisk2}}
@a $\{(x,y)\mid x^2+y^2>4\}$, everything outside the circle of radius 2 (not the circle itself).

@sim c-continuity-1
At what points $(x,y)$ is $f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{y^2-5y+6}$ continuous?
@s What could break?
A fraction: the bottom cannot be zero.
@s Factorise the bottom
Two numbers that multiply to $6$ and add to $-5$: $-2$ and $-3$.
$$y^2-5y+6=(y-2)(y-3)$$
@s Condition
$(y-2)(y-3)\neq0$, so $y\neq2$ and $y\neq3$.
@s Describe it
$y=2$ and $y=3$ are two horizontal lines. Remove them, keep everything else.
@a $\{(x,y)\mid y\neq 2 \text{ and } y\neq 3\}$, the plane without the lines $y=2$ and $y=3$.

@sim c-continuity-2
At what points $(x,y,z)$ is $f(x,y,z)=\sqrt{4-x^2-z^2}$ continuous?
@s What could break?
Square root: inside $\ge 0$.
$$4-x^2-z^2\ge 0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x^2+z^2\le 4$$
@s Describe it
In 3D, $x^2+z^2=4$ is a cylinder of radius $2$ around the $y$-axis (this time $y$ is the free letter).

$\le4$ means on the cylinder or inside it.
@a $\{(x,y,z)\mid x^2+z^2\le 4\}$, the solid cylinder of radius 2 around the $y$-axis, surface included.

@sim c-continuity-3
Find the continuous extension $g$ of $f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^3-3x^2-xy^2+3y^2}{x^3-3x^2+xy^2-3y^2+2x-6}$ at $(3,1)$.
@s Check the point
At $(3,1)$: top $=27-27-3+3=0$, bottom $=27-27+3-3+6-6=0$.

$\frac00$, so $f(3,1)$ is not defined. There is a hole.
@s Factorise the top
Group in pairs:
$$x^2(x-3)-y^2(x-3)=(x-3)(x^2-y^2)$$
@s Factorise the bottom
$$x^2(x-3)+y^2(x-3)+2(x-3)=(x-3)(x^2+y^2+2)$$
@s Cancel and take the limit
$$\lim_{(x,y)\to(3,1)}\frac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2+2}=\frac{9-1}{9+1+2}=\frac{8}{12}=\frac23$$
@s Fill the hole
$g=f$ everywhere else, and $g(3,1)=\tfrac23$.
@a $g(x,y)=f(x,y)$ for $(x,y)\neq(3,1)$, and $g(3,1)=\dfrac23$. (Same as $g=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{x^2+y^2+2}$.)
