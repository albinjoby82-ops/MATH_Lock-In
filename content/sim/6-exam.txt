@sim x-q1-0
Let $f(x,y)=\dfrac{\ln(y)}{\sqrt{9-x^2-y^2}}$. Determine the domain of $f$ in set notation.
@s Spot everything that could break
$\ln(y)$ → needs $y>0$.

Square root → inside $\ge0$.

Fraction → bottom $\neq0$.
@s Combine the root and the fraction
Strictly positive inside:
$$9-x^2-y^2>0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x^2+y^2<9$$
@s Put both conditions together
Both must be true at the same time ("and").
@a $\text{dom}(f)=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\mid y>0\ \text{and}\ x^2+y^2<9\}$

@sim x-q1-1
Sketch the domain $\{(x,y)\mid y>0\ \text{and}\ x^2+y^2<9\}$.
@s Draw each condition
$x^2+y^2<9$: inside the circle, centre $(0,0)$, radius $3$. Not the edge, so **dashed** circle.

$y>0$: above the $x$-axis. Not the axis itself, so **dashed** along the axis too.
@s Shade where both are true
The top half of the disk.
{{fig:halfdiskup}}
@a The open half-disk: centre $(0,0)$, radius $3$, above the $x$-axis, with no edges included.

@sim x-q1-2
For $f(x,y)=\dfrac{\ln(y)}{\sqrt{9-x^2-y^2}}$, determine $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,e)}f(x,y)$.
@s Is the point allowed?
$y=e>0$ ✓ and $0^2+e^2\approx7.39<9$ ✓. So $(0,e)$ is in the domain.
@s So just plug in
$f$ is continuous where it is defined, so the limit is just the value. Remember $\ln e=1$:
$$\frac{\ln e}{\sqrt{9-0-e^2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{9-e^2}}$$
@a $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{9-e^2}}$ (leave it exact).

@sim x-q2-0
Find $f_x$ and $f_y$ for $f(x,y)=\sqrt{\cos(x^3y^2)}$.
@s See the layers
$$\sqrt{\ \cdot\ }\quad\to\quad\cos(\ \cdot\ )\quad\to\quad x^3y^2$$
Differentiate each layer and multiply.
@s Layer by layer
Root: $(\sqrt u)'=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt u}$.

$\cos$: $\cos\to-\sin$.

Inside, in $x$: $x^3y^2\to3x^2y^2$.
@s Multiply for $f_x$
$$f_x=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\cos(x^3y^2)}}\cdot\left(-\sin(x^3y^2)\right)\cdot3x^2y^2=-\frac{3x^2y^2\sin(x^3y^2)}{2\sqrt{\cos(x^3y^2)}}$$
@s Same for $f_y$
Only the inside changes: in $y$, $x^3y^2\to2x^3y$.
$$f_y=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\cos(x^3y^2)}}\cdot\left(-\sin(x^3y^2)\right)\cdot2x^3y=-\frac{x^3y\sin(x^3y^2)}{\sqrt{\cos(x^3y^2)}}$$
@a $f_x=-\dfrac{3x^2y^2\sin(x^3y^2)}{2\sqrt{\cos(x^3y^2)}},\qquad f_y=-\dfrac{x^3y\sin(x^3y^2)}{\sqrt{\cos(x^3y^2)}}$

@sim x-q2-1
Use the chain rule to find $w'(1)$ if $w=\cos(xyz)$ and $x=1+t$, $y=2t$, $z=t^2-2$.
@s Chain rule
$$w'(t)=w_x\,x'+w_y\,y'+w_z\,z'$$
@s The pieces
$$w_x=-yz\sin(xyz)\qquad w_y=-xz\sin(xyz)\qquad w_z=-xy\sin(xyz)$$
$$x'=1\qquad y'=2\qquad z'=2t$$
@s Find everything at $t=1$
$x=2,\ y=2,\ z=-1$, so $xyz=-4$.

$yz=-2,\quad xz=-2,\quad xy=4$

$x'=1,\quad y'=2,\quad z'=2$
@s Multiply and add
$$w'(1)=-\sin(-4)\,\big[(-2)(1)+(-2)(2)+(4)(2)\big]=-\sin(-4)\cdot2$$
$\sin(-4)=-\sin4$, so this is $2\sin4$.
@a $w'(1)=2\sin4$

@sim x-q2-2
$x=r^2-s^2$, $y=\sin(rs)$. Find $w_x$ if $r=2$, $s=\tfrac\pi2$, $w_r=12$, $w_s=-3\pi$.
@s Chain rule, twice
$$w_r=w_x\,x_r+w_y\,y_r\qquad w_s=w_x\,x_s+w_y\,y_s$$
@s The four small derivatives
$x_r=2r,\quad x_s=-2s$

$y_r=s\cos(rs),\quad y_s=r\cos(rs)$
@s Put in $r=2$, $s=\pi/2$
$rs=\pi$ and $\cos\pi=-1$:

$x_r=4,\quad x_s=-\pi,\quad y_r=-\tfrac\pi2,\quad y_s=-2$
@s Two equations
$$12=4w_x-\tfrac\pi2w_y\qquad\text{(i)}$$
$$-3\pi=-\pi w_x-2w_y\qquad\text{(ii)}$$
@s Get rid of $w_y$
Multiply (i) by $\tfrac4\pi$ so it has $-2w_y$ like (ii):
$$\tfrac{48}{\pi}=\tfrac{16}{\pi}w_x-2w_y$$
Subtract (ii):
$$\tfrac{48}{\pi}+3\pi=\left(\tfrac{16}{\pi}+\pi\right)w_x$$
@s Spot the shortcut
The left side is exactly $3\times$ the bracket. So $w_x=3$.

Check in (i): $12=12-\tfrac\pi2w_y$, so $w_y=0$. ✓
@a $w_x=3$

@sim x-q3-0
$2xyz+e^{x+y+z}=-11$ defines $z(x,y)$. Find $z_x$ and $z_y$ at $(1,2,-3)$.
@s Differentiate in $x$ ($y$ is a constant)
$2xyz$: $2y$ is a number, and $x\cdot z$ needs the product rule → $2y(z+x\,z_x)$.

$e^{x+y+z}$: inside gives $1+z_x$ → $e^{x+y+z}(1+z_x)$.
$$2y(z+x\,z_x)+e^{x+y+z}(1+z_x)=0$$
@s Solve for $z_x$
$$z_x\left(2xy+e^{x+y+z}\right)=-\left(2yz+e^{x+y+z}\right)$$
$$z_x=-\frac{2yz+e^{x+y+z}}{2xy+e^{x+y+z}}$$
@s Plug in the point
$x+y+z=0$, so $e^0=1$. Also $2yz=-12$, $2xy=4$:
$$z_x=-\frac{-12+1}{4+1}=\frac{11}{5}$$
@s $z_y$ the same way
Swap the roles of $x$ and $y$:
$$z_y=-\frac{2xz+e^{x+y+z}}{2xy+e^{x+y+z}}=-\frac{-6+1}{4+1}=1$$
@a $z_x=\dfrac{11}{5},\qquad z_y=1$

@sim x-q3-1
$f_1=2x+y+3z$, $f_2=e^x+e^y$, $f_3=\ln(xyz)$. (i) Find the Jacobian matrix. (ii) Find its determinant at $(1,1,1)$.
@s Tidy $f_3$ first
$\ln(xyz)=\ln x+\ln y+\ln z$, so its partials are $\tfrac1x,\ \tfrac1y,\ \tfrac1z$.
@s Fill the grid
Row 1 ($f_1$): $2,\ 1,\ 3$

Row 2 ($f_2$): $e^x,\ e^y,\ 0$ (no $z$ in it, so $0$ last)

Row 3 ($f_3$): $\tfrac1x,\ \tfrac1y,\ \tfrac1z$
$$J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&3\\e^x&e^y&0\\ \frac1x&\frac1y&\frac1z\end{pmatrix}$$
@s At $(1,1,1)$
$$J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&3\\e&e&0\\1&1&1\end{pmatrix}$$
@s Expand along the top row
$$2\,(e\cdot1-0\cdot1)-1\,(e\cdot1-0\cdot1)+3\,(e\cdot1-e\cdot1)$$
$$=2e-e+0=e$$
@a $J$ as above; $\det J(1,1,1)=e$

@sim x-q3-2
$E=\tfrac12mv^2$. Find the approximate percentage change in $E$ if $m$ increases by 3% and $v$ decreases by 2%.
@s Powers become multipliers
$\tfrac12$ is a constant. $m$ has power $1$, $v$ has power $2$:
$$\%E\approx1(\%m)+2(\%v)$$
@s Put the numbers in
Increase → $+3$. Decrease → $-2$.
$$\%E\approx3+2(-2)=3-4=-1$$
@a About a $1\%$ decrease.

@sim x-q4-0
$T(x,y,z)=e^{2x^2-y^2-z^2}$. Find $\nabla T$ at $P=(1,1,1)$.
@s Partials (chain rule on $e$)
Each one keeps the same $e$ part, times the derivative of the exponent:
$$\nabla T=e^{2x^2-y^2-z^2}\,\langle4x,\ -2y,\ -2z\rangle$$
@s At $P$
Exponent: $2-1-1=0$, so $e^0=1$.
$$\nabla T=\langle4,\ -2,\ -2\rangle$$
@a $\nabla T(P)=\langle4,-2,-2\rangle$

@sim x-q4-1
Find a unit vector $\mathbf u$ pointing from $P=(1,1,1)$ towards $Q=(3,2,-1)$.
@s Arrow from P to Q
Subtract $P$ from $Q$:
$$\mathbf v=\langle3-1,\ 2-1,\ -1-1\rangle=\langle2,\ 1,\ -2\rangle$$
@s Length
$$\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{4+1+4}=3$$
@a $\mathbf u=\dfrac13\langle2,1,-2\rangle$

@sim x-q4-2
With $\nabla T(P)=\langle4,-2,-2\rangle$ and $\mathbf u=\tfrac13\langle2,1,-2\rangle$, find the rate of change of $T$ at $P$ in the direction of $\mathbf u$.
@s It's the directional derivative
$$D_{\mathbf u}T=\nabla T\cdot\mathbf u$$
@s Dot product
$$\frac13\langle4,-2,-2\rangle\cdot\langle2,1,-2\rangle=\frac{8-2+4}{3}$$
@a $\dfrac{10}{3}$ (temperature is rising in that direction)

@sim x-q4-3
With $\nabla T(P)=\langle4,-2,-2\rangle$: (d) In which direction does $T$ increase fastest at $P$? (e) What is that maximum rate?
@s Fastest increase
Always the direction of the gradient: $\langle4,-2,-2\rangle$.
@s Maximum rate
The length of the gradient:
$$\|\nabla T\|=\sqrt{16+4+4}=\sqrt{24}=2\sqrt6$$
@a (d) Direction $\langle4,-2,-2\rangle$. (e) Maximum rate $2\sqrt6$.

@sim x-q5-0
Find the tangent plane to $\sin(2x+y+z)=0$ at $Q=\left(\pi,\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2\right)$.
@s Gradient
Chain rule. The inside $2x+y+z$ gives $2,1,1$:
$$\nabla F=\cos(2x+y+z)\,\langle2,\ 1,\ 1\rangle$$
@s At Q
$2x+y+z=2\pi+\tfrac\pi2+\tfrac\pi2=3\pi$.

$\cos3\pi$: take away $2\pi$ (one full turn) to get $\cos\pi=-1$.
$$\nabla F=\langle-2,\ -1,\ -1\rangle$$
@s Plane formula
$$-2(x-\pi)-\left(y-\tfrac\pi2\right)-\left(z-\tfrac\pi2\right)=0$$
@s Tidy
Multiply by $-1$: $\ 2x+y+z=2\pi+\tfrac\pi2+\tfrac\pi2=3\pi$
@a $2x+y+z=3\pi$

@sim x-q5-1
$f=-3x^2-y^2-2z^2-xy+5x-y+8z$. (i) Find the critical point $P$. (ii) Find the leading principal minors of the Hessian. (iii) Max, min or saddle?
@s First derivatives
$$f_x=-6x-y+5\qquad f_y=-2y-x-1\qquad f_z=-4z+8$$
@s Solve
$f_z=0\Rightarrow z=2$.

$f_y=0\Rightarrow x=-2y-1$.

Put into $f_x=0$: $\ -6(-2y-1)-y+5=11y+11=0\Rightarrow y=-1$, then $x=1$.

$P=(1,-1,2)$.
@s Hessian
$$H=\begin{pmatrix}-6&-1&0\\-1&-2&0\\0&0&-4\end{pmatrix}$$
@s Leading principal minors
$|H_1|=-6$

$|H_2|=(-6)(-2)-(-1)(-1)=11$

$|H_3|$: the last row is $0,0,-4$, so $|H_3|=-4\times|H_2|=-44$
@s Read the signs
$-,\ +,\ -$ → $H$ is negative definite → maximum.
@a $P=(1,-1,2)$; minors $-6,\ 11,\ -44$; $P$ is a local maximum.

@sim x-q6-0
Find the max and min of $f=xy^2z+2$ subject to $z=2$ and $x^2+y^2+z^2=16$.
@s Gradients
$\nabla f=\langle y^2z,\ 2xyz,\ xy^2\rangle$

$g_1=z$: $\ \nabla g_1=\langle0,0,1\rangle$

$g_2=x^2+y^2+z^2$: $\ \nabla g_2=\langle2x,2y,2z\rangle$
@s Lagrange equations
$\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g_1+\mu\nabla g_2$, and put $z=2$ straight in:
$$2y^2=2\mu x\qquad(1)$$
$$4xy=2\mu y\qquad(2)$$
$$xy^2=\lambda+4\mu\qquad(3)$$
(3) only tells us $\lambda$, which we don't need.
@s Factor (2)
$$y(4x-2\mu)=0\ \Rightarrow\ y=0\ \text{ or }\ \mu=2x$$
@s Case $y=0$
Constraint: $x^2+0+4=16\Rightarrow x=\pm\sqrt{12}$. Then $f=0+2=2$.
@s Case $\mu=2x$
(1) becomes $2y^2=4x^2$, so $y^2=2x^2$. Constraint: $x^2+2x^2+4=16\Rightarrow x^2=4\Rightarrow x=\pm2$, and $y^2=8$.

$f=xy^2z+2=x\cdot8\cdot2+2=16x+2=\pm32+2$.
@s Compare all values
$2$, $\ 34$, $\ -30$.
@a Maximum $34$ (at $(2,\pm2\sqrt2,2)$). Minimum $-30$ (at $(-2,\pm2\sqrt2,2)$).

@sim x-q6-1
Find the general solution of $y''+y=t^2+\cos2t$.
@s Step 1: $y_h$
$$r^2+1=0\Rightarrow r=\pm i$$
Complex with $\alpha=0$, $\beta=1$:
$$y_h=C_1\cos t+C_2\sin t$$
@s Step 2: guess $y_p$
Right side = degree 2 polynomial + $\cos2t$:
$$y_p=At^2+Bt+C+D\cos2t+E\sin2t$$
No clash: $\cos2t$ is not $\cos t$.
@s Differentiate twice
$$y_p''=2A-4D\cos2t-4E\sin2t$$
@s Put it in
$$y_p''+y_p=At^2+Bt+(2A+C)-3D\cos2t-3E\sin2t$$
This must equal $t^2+\cos2t$.
@s Match coefficients
$t^2$: $A=1$

$t$: $B=0$

number: $2A+C=0\Rightarrow C=-2$

$\cos2t$: $-3D=1\Rightarrow D=-\tfrac13$

$\sin2t$: $-3E=0\Rightarrow E=0$
@s Add them
$$y=y_h+y_p$$
@a $y=C_1\cos t+C_2\sin t+t^2-2-\dfrac13\cos2t$
