@@topic x-q1 | Exam Q1: domain, sketch, limit | Sample exam · Q1
@learn
**The real exam:** 2 hours, 6 questions, all equal marks. That's about **20 minutes per question**.

**Rules that cost marks if you forget them:**

Leave answers **exact** ($\sqrt{14}$, $\cos2$, $\tfrac{11\pi}{2}$), not decimals.

**Show all working.**

**No formula sheet**, so the formulas in the Learn boxes need to be in your head.

**How to use this section:** do each question on paper with a timer first, then check it step by step.

**This question uses:** Domains, Limits.
@check What 2 things can make a point not allowed in the domain?
@checka Dividing by zero, and a negative inside a square root (and $\ln$ of something $\le0$).

@q Sample exam · Q1(a)
Let $f(x,y)=\dfrac{\ln(x)}{\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}}$. Determine the domain of $f$ in set notation.
@s Spot everything that could break
Three things to check:

$\ln(x)$ → needs $x>0$.

Square root → inside $\ge0$.

Fraction → bottom $\neq0$.
@s Combine the root and the fraction
Inside the root must be $\ge0$ and the whole root $\neq0$, so strictly positive:
$$4-x^2-y^2>0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ x^2+y^2<4$$
@s Put both conditions together
Both must be true at the same time ("and").
@a $\text{dom}(f)=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2\mid x>0\ \text{and}\ x^2+y^2<4\}$

@q Sample exam · Q1(b)
Sketch the domain from part (a).
@s Draw each condition
$x^2+y^2<4$: inside the circle, centre $(0,0)$, radius $2$. Not the edge, so **dashed** circle.

$x>0$: right of the $y$-axis. Not the axis itself, so **dashed** along the axis too.
@s Shade where both are true
The right half of the disk.
{{fig:halfdisk}}
@a The open half-disk: centre $(0,0)$, radius $2$, to the right of the $y$-axis, with no edges included.

@q Sample exam · Q1(c)
Determine $\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(1,0)}f(x,y)$.
@s Is the point allowed?
$x=1>0$ ✓ and $1^2+0^2=1<4$ ✓. So $(1,0)$ is in the domain.
@s So just plug in
$f$ is made of continuous pieces, so where it is defined the limit is just the value:
$$\frac{\ln1}{\sqrt{4-1-0}}=\frac{0}{\sqrt3}=0$$
@a $0$ (because $(1,0)$ is in the domain and $f$ is continuous there).


@@topic x-q2 | Exam Q2: partials and chain rule | Sample exam · Q2
@learn
**This question uses:** Partial derivatives, Chain rule.

Part (c) is the hard one: it gives you numbers for $w_r$ and $w_s$ and asks for $w_x$. Write the two chain rule equations, put the numbers in, and you get two simultaneous equations for $w_x$ and $w_y$.
@check If $w=w(x,y)$ with $x$ and $y$ depending on $r$, what is $w_r$?
@checka $w_r=w_x\,x_r+w_y\,y_r$.

@q Sample exam · Q2(a)
Find $f_x$ and $f_y$ for $f(x,y)=\sqrt{\sin(x^2y^3)}$.
@s See the layers
Three layers, outside to inside:
$$\sqrt{\ \cdot\ }\quad\to\quad\sin(\ \cdot\ )\quad\to\quad x^2y^3$$
Chain rule = differentiate each layer and multiply.
@s Layer by layer
Root: $(\sqrt u)'=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt u}$.

$\sin$: $\sin\to\cos$.

Inside, in $x$: $x^2y^3\to2xy^3$.
@s Multiply for $f_x$
$$f_x=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\sin(x^2y^3)}}\cdot\cos(x^2y^3)\cdot2xy^3=\frac{xy^3\cos(x^2y^3)}{\sqrt{\sin(x^2y^3)}}$$
@s Same for $f_y$
Only the inside changes: in $y$, $x^2y^3\to3x^2y^2$.
$$f_y=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\sin(x^2y^3)}}\cdot\cos(x^2y^3)\cdot3x^2y^2=\frac{3x^2y^2\cos(x^2y^3)}{2\sqrt{\sin(x^2y^3)}}$$
@a $f_x=\dfrac{xy^3\cos(x^2y^3)}{\sqrt{\sin(x^2y^3)}},\qquad f_y=\dfrac{3x^2y^2\cos(x^2y^3)}{2\sqrt{\sin(x^2y^3)}}$

@q Sample exam · Q2(b)
Use the chain rule to find $w'(1)$ if $w=\sin(xyz)$ and $x=1+t^2$, $y=1-2t$, $z=t^3-2$.
@s Chain rule
$$w'(t)=w_x\,x'+w_y\,y'+w_z\,z'$$
@s The pieces
$$w_x=yz\cos(xyz)\qquad w_y=xz\cos(xyz)\qquad w_z=xy\cos(xyz)$$
$$x'=2t\qquad y'=-2\qquad z'=3t^2$$
@s Find everything at $t=1$
$x=2,\ y=-1,\ z=-1$, so $xyz=2$.

$yz=1,\quad xz=-2,\quad xy=-2$

$x'=2,\quad y'=-2,\quad z'=3$
@s Multiply and add
$$w'(1)=\cos2\,\big[(1)(2)+(-2)(-2)+(-2)(3)\big]=\cos2\,(2+4-6)$$
@a $w'(1)=0$

@q Sample exam · Q2(c)
$x=r^2+s^2$, $y=\cos(rs)$. Find $w_x$ if $r=2$, $s=\tfrac\pi4$, $w_r=8$, $w_s=\pi$.
@s Chain rule, twice
$$w_r=w_x\,x_r+w_y\,y_r\qquad w_s=w_x\,x_s+w_y\,y_s$$
@s The four small derivatives
$x_r=2r,\quad x_s=2s$

$y_r=-s\sin(rs),\quad y_s=-r\sin(rs)$
@s Put in $r=2$, $s=\pi/4$
$rs=\tfrac\pi2$ and $\sin\tfrac\pi2=1$:

$x_r=4,\quad x_s=\tfrac\pi2,\quad y_r=-\tfrac\pi4,\quad y_s=-2$
@s Two equations
$$8=4w_x-\tfrac\pi4w_y\qquad\text{(i)}$$
$$\pi=\tfrac\pi2w_x-2w_y\qquad\text{(ii)}$$
@s Get rid of $w_y$
Multiply (i) by $\tfrac8\pi$ so it has $-2w_y$ like (ii):
$$\tfrac{64}{\pi}=\tfrac{32}{\pi}w_x-2w_y$$
Subtract (ii):
$$\tfrac{64}{\pi}-\pi=\left(\tfrac{32}{\pi}-\tfrac\pi2\right)w_x$$
@s Spot the shortcut
The left side is exactly $2\times$ the bracket: $2\left(\tfrac{32}{\pi}-\tfrac\pi2\right)=\tfrac{64}\pi-\pi$. So $w_x=2$.

Check in (i): $8=8-\tfrac\pi4w_y$, so $w_y=0$. ✓
@a $w_x=2$


@@topic x-q3 | Exam Q3: implicit, Jacobian, % error | Sample exam · Q3
@learn
**This question uses:** Implicit differentiation, Jacobian matrix, Linear approximation and % error.

Three separate mini-questions. Each one is a recipe you can learn.
@check Percentage error shortcut for $Q=k\,a^2b^3$?
@checka $\%Q\approx2(\%a)+3(\%b)$.

@q Sample exam · Q3(a)
$xyz+e^{x+y+z}=3$ defines $z(x,y)$. Find $z_x$ and $z_y$ at $(2,-1,-1)$.
@s Differentiate in $x$ ($y$ is a constant)
$xyz$: $y$ is a number, and $x\cdot z$ needs the product rule → $y(z+x\,z_x)$.

$e^{x+y+z}$: chain rule, inside gives $1+z_x$ → $e^{x+y+z}(1+z_x)$.
$$y(z+x\,z_x)+e^{x+y+z}(1+z_x)=0$$
@s Solve for $z_x$
Expand and collect the $z_x$ terms:
$$z_x\left(xy+e^{x+y+z}\right)=-\left(yz+e^{x+y+z}\right)$$
$$z_x=-\frac{yz+e^{x+y+z}}{xy+e^{x+y+z}}$$
@s Plug in the point
$x+y+z=0$, so $e^0=1$. Also $yz=1$, $xy=-2$:
$$z_x=-\frac{1+1}{-2+1}=-\frac{2}{-1}=2$$
@s $z_y$ the same way
The equation looks the same if you swap $x$ and $y$, so swap them in the answer:
$$z_y=-\frac{xz+e^{x+y+z}}{xy+e^{x+y+z}}=-\frac{-2+1}{-2+1}=-1$$
@a $z_x=2,\qquad z_y=-1$

@q Sample exam · Q3(b)
$f_1=x+2y+3z$, $f_2=e^y+e^z$, $f_3=\ln(xyz)$. (i) Find the Jacobian matrix. (ii) Find its determinant at $(1,1,1)$.
@s Tidy $f_3$ first
$\ln(xyz)=\ln x+\ln y+\ln z$, so its partials are $\tfrac1x,\ \tfrac1y,\ \tfrac1z$.
@s Fill the grid
Row 1 ($f_1$): $1,\ 2,\ 3$

Row 2 ($f_2$): $0,\ e^y,\ e^z$ (no $x$ in it, so $0$ first)

Row 3 ($f_3$): $\tfrac1x,\ \tfrac1y,\ \tfrac1z$
$$J=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\0&e^y&e^z\\ \frac1x&\frac1y&\frac1z\end{pmatrix}$$
@s At $(1,1,1)$
$$J=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\0&e&e\\1&1&1\end{pmatrix}$$
@s Expand along the top row
$$1\,(e\cdot1-e\cdot1)-2\,(0\cdot1-e\cdot1)+3\,(0\cdot1-e\cdot1)$$
$$=0+2e-3e=-e$$
@a $J$ as above; $\det J(1,1,1)=-e$

@q Sample exam · Q3(c)
$L=4\pi R^2\sigma T^4$. Find the approximate percentage error in $L$ if $R$ increases by 4% and $T$ decreases by 1%.
@s Powers become multipliers
$4\pi$ and $\sigma$ are constants. $R$ has power $2$, $T$ has power $4$:
$$\%L\approx2(\%R)+4(\%T)$$
@s Put the numbers in
Increase → $+4$. Decrease → $-1$.
$$\%L\approx2(4)+4(-1)=8-4=4$$
@a About a $4\%$ increase.


@@topic x-q4 | Exam Q4: gradient and directions | Sample exam · Q4
@learn
**This question uses:** Gradient and directional derivative.

New bit: **a unit vector from point $P$ towards point $Q$**. Subtract to get the arrow, then divide by its length:
$$\mathbf v=Q-P\qquad\mathbf u=\frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}$$
@check Arrow from $P=(1,1)$ to $Q=(4,5)$, as a unit vector?
@checka $\mathbf v=\langle3,4\rangle$, length $5$, so $\mathbf u=\left\langle\tfrac35,\tfrac45\right\rangle$.

@q Sample exam · Q4(a)
$T(x,y,z)=e^{x^2-4y^2-9z^2}$. Find $\nabla T$ at $P=(2,-1,0)$.
@s Partials (chain rule on $e$)
$e^{\text{stuff}}\to e^{\text{stuff}}\times(\text{stuff})'$. Each one keeps the same $e$ part:
$$\nabla T=e^{x^2-4y^2-9z^2}\,\langle2x,\ -8y,\ -18z\rangle$$
@s At $P$
Exponent: $4-4-0=0$, so $e^0=1$.
$$\nabla T=\langle4,\ 8,\ 0\rangle$$
@a $\nabla T(P)=\langle4,8,0\rangle$

@q Sample exam · Q4(b)
Find a unit vector $\mathbf u$ pointing from $P=(2,-1,0)$ towards $Q=(3,-3,3)$.
@s Arrow from P to Q
Subtract $P$ from $Q$:
$$\mathbf v=\langle3-2,\ -3-(-1),\ 3-0\rangle=\langle1,\ -2,\ 3\rangle$$
@s Length
$$\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{1+4+9}=\sqrt{14}$$
@a $\mathbf u=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{14}}\langle1,-2,3\rangle$

@q Sample exam · Q4(c)
Find the rate of change of $T$ at $P$ in the direction of $\mathbf u$.
@s It's the directional derivative
$$D_{\mathbf u}T=\nabla T\cdot\mathbf u$$
@s Dot product
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{14}}\langle4,8,0\rangle\cdot\langle1,-2,3\rangle=\frac{4-16+0}{\sqrt{14}}$$
@a $-\dfrac{12}{\sqrt{14}}$ (temperature is falling in that direction)

@q Sample exam · Q4(d) and (e)
(d) In which direction does $T$ increase fastest at $P$? (e) What is that maximum rate?
@s Fastest increase
Always the direction of the gradient: $\langle4,8,0\rangle$.
@s Maximum rate
The length of the gradient:
$$\|\nabla T\|=\sqrt{16+64+0}=\sqrt{80}=4\sqrt5$$
@a (d) Direction $\langle4,8,0\rangle$. (e) Maximum rate $4\sqrt5$.


@@topic x-q5 | Exam Q5: tangent plane, 3-variable max/min | Sample exam · Q5
@learn
**This question uses:** Tangent lines and planes, Critical points with 3 variables.

Part (b) is the full 3-variable recipe: critical point → Hessian → leading principal minors → read the signs.
@check Signs $+,+,+$ mean?
@checka Minimum.

@q Sample exam · Q5(a)
Find the tangent plane to $\cos(x+2y+3z)=0$ at $Q=\left(\tfrac\pi2,\pi,\pi\right)$.
@s Gradient
Chain rule. The inside $x+2y+3z$ gives $1,2,3$:
$$\nabla F=-\sin(x+2y+3z)\,\langle1,\ 2,\ 3\rangle$$
@s At Q
$x+2y+3z=\tfrac\pi2+2\pi+3\pi=\tfrac{11\pi}{2}$.

$\sin\tfrac{11\pi}{2}$: take away $4\pi$ (two full turns) to get $\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2}=-1$.

So $-\sin=+1$ and
$$\nabla F=\langle1,\ 2,\ 3\rangle$$
@s Plane formula
$$1\left(x-\tfrac\pi2\right)+2(y-\pi)+3(z-\pi)=0$$
@s Tidy
$x+2y+3z=\tfrac\pi2+2\pi+3\pi=\tfrac{11\pi}2$
@a $x+2y+3z=\dfrac{11\pi}{2}$

@q Sample exam · Q5(b)
$f=-2x^2-y^2-4z^2-yz+4x-y+7z$. (i) Find the critical point $P$. (ii) Find the leading principal minors of the Hessian. (iii) Max, min or saddle?
@s First derivatives
$$f_x=-4x+4\qquad f_y=-2y-z-1\qquad f_z=-8z-y+7$$
@s Solve
$f_x=0\Rightarrow x=1$.

$f_y=0\Rightarrow z=-2y-1$.

Put into $f_z=0$: $\ -8(-2y-1)-y+7=15y+15=0\Rightarrow y=-1$, then $z=1$.

$P=(1,-1,1)$.
@s Hessian
$$H=\begin{pmatrix}-4&0&0\\0&-2&-1\\0&-1&-8\end{pmatrix}$$
@s Leading principal minors
$|H_1|=-4$

$|H_2|=(-4)(-2)-0=8$

$|H_3|=-4\big[(-2)(-8)-(-1)(-1)\big]=-4(16-1)=-60$
@s Read the signs
$-,\ +,\ -$ → $H$ is negative definite → maximum.
@a $P=(1,-1,1)$; minors $-4,\ 8,\ -60$; $P$ is a local maximum.


@@topic x-q6 | Exam Q6: Lagrange and an ODE | Sample exam · Q6
@learn
**This question uses:** Lagrange multipliers (two constraints), Second order with right side ≠ 0.

For (a): one constraint is just $z=1$, so put $z=1$ in early. It makes everything easier.

For (b): the roots are complex with no real part ($\pm2i$), so $y_h$ is just cos and sin.
@check Roots $\pm2i$. What is $y_h$?
@checka $C_1\cos2t+C_2\sin2t$ (real part $0$, so no $e$ in front).

@q Sample exam · Q6(a)
Find the max and min of $f=x^2yz+1$ subject to $z=1$ and $x^2+y^2+z^2=10$.
@s Gradients
$\nabla f=\langle2xyz,\ x^2z,\ x^2y\rangle$

$g_1=z$: $\ \nabla g_1=\langle0,0,1\rangle$

$g_2=x^2+y^2+z^2$: $\ \nabla g_2=\langle2x,2y,2z\rangle$
@s Lagrange equations
$\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g_1+\mu\nabla g_2$, and put $z=1$ straight in:
$$2xy=2\mu x\qquad(1)$$
$$x^2=2\mu y\qquad(2)$$
$$x^2y=\lambda+2\mu\qquad(3)$$
(3) only tells us $\lambda$, which we don't need.
@s Factor (1)
$$x(y-\mu)=0\ \Rightarrow\ x=0\ \text{ or }\ y=\mu$$
@s Case $x=0$
Constraint: $0+y^2+1=10\Rightarrow y=\pm3$. Then $f=0+1=1$.
@s Case $y=\mu$
(2) becomes $x^2=2y^2$. Constraint: $2y^2+y^2+1=10\Rightarrow y^2=3\Rightarrow y=\pm\sqrt3$, and $x^2=6$.

$f=x^2y+1=6y+1=\pm6\sqrt3+1$.
@s Compare all values
$1$, $\ 1+6\sqrt3$, $\ 1-6\sqrt3$.
@a Maximum $1+6\sqrt3$ (at $(\pm\sqrt6,\sqrt3,1)$). Minimum $1-6\sqrt3$ (at $(\pm\sqrt6,-\sqrt3,1)$).

@q Sample exam · Q6(b)
Find the general solution of $y''+4y=t^2+\cos t$.
@s Step 1: $y_h$
$$r^2+4=0\Rightarrow r=\pm2i$$
Complex with $\alpha=0$, $\beta=2$:
$$y_h=C_1\cos2t+C_2\sin2t$$
@s Step 2: guess $y_p$
Right side = degree 2 polynomial + $\cos t$:
$$y_p=At^2+Bt+C+D\cos t+E\sin t$$
No clash: $\cos t$ is not $\cos2t$.
@s Differentiate twice
$$y_p''=2A-D\cos t-E\sin t$$
@s Put it in
$$y_p''+4y_p=4At^2+4Bt+(2A+4C)+3D\cos t+3E\sin t$$
This must equal $t^2+\cos t$.
@s Match coefficients
$t^2$: $4A=1\Rightarrow A=\tfrac14$

$t$: $4B=0\Rightarrow B=0$

number: $2A+4C=0\Rightarrow C=-\tfrac18$

$\cos t$: $3D=1\Rightarrow D=\tfrac13$

$\sin t$: $3E=0\Rightarrow E=0$
@s Add them
$$y=y_h+y_p$$
@a $y=C_1\cos2t+C_2\sin2t+\dfrac{t^2}{4}-\dfrac18+\dfrac13\cos t$
